Notes on working with agents

Head of Agents.
Not a faster typist.

What it actually feels like to be the "10x dev", the agent manager, the person running five projects before lunch.

People say 10x like it means typing ten times faster. It doesn't. With agents doing the typing, your hands stopped being the limit a while ago. The new limit is your head.

So 10x is a different shape of work. You are doing many things at once. Each one stays simple for you, because the complicated part lives somewhere else.

10x is not more output per project.
It is more projects per head.

The picture

Draw two arrows growing up the page. One for you, one for the agent. Next to each arrow, a box: the context that piles up on that side as work happens. Every new project stacks another row, and both arrows grow with it.

When you micromanage, your box fills with the agent's work. Variable names, import errors, which test to retry. Each row gets tall. By project three you are past the line, and the agent's box sits mostly empty, waiting for your next message.

When you delegate goals, the rows on your side stay thin: why this exists, what shape it should take, how you'll know it's done. The heavy context moves to the agent's side, where it belongs. Your box has room for eight rows instead of two.

Same person. Same agents. The only thing that changed is where the context lives.

Two ways to get stuck

Two numbers matter: how many things you manage, and how long you can keep steering each one. Most of us hit a wall on one of them.

Micromanaging caps how many. You can steer one project for a long time, because you are in every detail. But every detail is yours to carry, so the second and third projects don't fit.

Vibe coding caps how long. It flies for the first hour. Then decisions pile up, and they live in two bad places: old prompts and your head. The agent forgets one, you correct it, it forgets another. You end up fighting the agent prompt by prompt, and the project stalls.

The fix for both is the same. Put the decisions in one central place that you and the agent both read. A decision repo is that shared abstraction. It gives a vibe coder enough control to steer the project for longer, and it lets a micromanager stop carrying the details.

Scale yourself

Doing more by doing less.

You can't grow your head. You can change what goes into it. Two moves:

  • HoldMany simple things. A 10x head is a list of short, clear rows. Not one deep, tangled one. If a project needs you to remember forty details, it is designed wrong.
  • GuardStop attention leaking. Every "quick look" at a diff you didn't need to see, every ping you answer about a detail the agent could decide, drains the budget for the other rows.

Think like an executive

An executive doesn't rewrite the slide deck. They decide what the deck needs to convince people of, and review it when it's done. Delegating goals is the norm. Diving into low-level details is the exception, and they notice when they're doing it.

Add a retry loop to
charge() with 3 attempts,
exponential backoff, and
log on final failure.
A goal
Failed card charges
shouldn't lose us customers.
Transient errors recover
on their own. Done when
dunning tickets drop.

The task fixes one function. The goal hands over a problem, and the agent can find the five places it actually shows up. You stay at the altitude of why and done. The agent owns how.

Design the division of labour

This doesn't happen by accident. Left alone, every session drifts toward micromanagement, because the prompt is the only place your decisions can live. So you design the split up front:

  • You keepDecisions. What we're building, what we're not, the calls already made and why.
  • Agents getExecution, with real autonomy inside those decisions. Not unlimited. Enough that they don't need to ask.
  • SharedA central decision repo both sides read. It makes your decisions quick, because the context is already there. It makes the agent's execution sound, because the direction is already there.

That shared layer is the thin band in the middle of the picture. Without it, the only bridge between the two columns is you, typing. With it, each row connects on its own.

You decide. Agents execute. The shared context connects them.
